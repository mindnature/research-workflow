# Research modes

Choose the mode that matches the requested deliverable. Modes can be combined;
the combination should be explicit in the output so the reader knows what was
and was not assessed.

## 1. Landscape and gap reconnaissance

Use when the user is starting from a topic, wants to understand a field, or is
looking for promising research questions.

Produce:

- a one-sentence scope and operational definition of the topic;
- major schools, theories, methods, populations, settings, or technical
  approaches;
- areas of convergence and live disagreement;
- a prioritized gap list using theoretical, empirical, methodological,
  contextual, temporal, and practice/policy dimensions;
- candidate questions with population/context, mechanism or comparison,
  outcome, and a feasible evidence path.

Do not call an under-searched topic a gap without checking whether the search
was narrow, the terminology varies, or evidence is inaccessible.

## 2. Literature matrix and comparative synthesis

Use when the user supplies papers or asks for a review of a body of literature.
Extract one study per row before drafting prose. At minimum compare question,
framework, unit of analysis, context/sample/data, design, measurement or
analytic approach, finding, limitation, and relevance to the user's question.

After the matrix, report patterns across studies rather than repeating one
abstract at a time. Explain whether apparent agreement reflects genuinely
similar designs or merely similar wording.

## 3. Concept, theory, and variable mapping

Use when the user needs a conceptual framework, hypotheses, a causal model, a
qualitative sensitizing framework, or construct clarification.

For every important construct, distinguish:

- definition and theoretical origin;
- observable indicator, measure, or coding approach;
- role: antecedent, outcome, mediator, moderator, control, mechanism, condition,
  or context;
- unit and level: individual, group, organization, system, text, artifact,
  event, or other appropriate unit;
- direction and strength of the proposed relationship;
- evidence supporting the relationship and its boundary conditions.

Do not convert correlation into causation merely because an arrow is drawn.
For qualitative or theoretical work, use relationships, propositions, processes,
or mechanisms instead of forcing everything into statistical variable language.

## 4. Contradiction detection and adversarial review

Use when findings conflict, a claim seems too confident, or the user asks for a
critical review. Build a contradiction map with the claim, evidence supporting
it, contrary evidence, study differences, plausible explanations, and the test
that would distinguish those explanations.

Then act as a demanding but fair reviewer. Ask:

- What is the strongest evidence against the conclusion?
- What alternative explanation remains viable?
- Which assumption is carrying the argument?
- What result would falsify or seriously weaken it?
- Does the design support the stated level of causal, predictive, theoretical,
  or practical inference?

End with a synthesis that preserves warranted strengths and narrows unsupported
claims. Do not manufacture disagreement for rhetorical balance.

## 5. Source and claim evidence audit

Use when accuracy, citation quality, policy implications, or publication risk
matters. Break the draft into checkable claims and link each important claim to
the precise source, evidence location, source type, and support strength.

Check whether:

- the source is primary and appropriate for the claim;
- the evidence actually entails the wording used;
- the source's population, setting, date, and method match the claim;
- corroborating sources are independent;
- limitations, retractions, corrections, conflicts, and missing data matter;
- certainty and causal language are calibrated.

Mark claims as verified, partially supported, unsupported, contradicted, or
requiring access/verification.

## 6. Plain-language explanation and teaching translation

Use when the user wants to understand or teach a complex concept. Preserve the
technical definition, then add an intuitive explanation, a carefully bounded
analogy, a concrete example, common confusion, and a short check for
understanding. Label analogies as analogies and do not let a memorable example
stand in for evidence.

## 7. Research design, proposal, or next-study planning

Use when findings must become an executable study or proposal. Connect each
research question to a suitable design, data source or sampling logic,
measurement/coding plan, analysis or interpretive procedure, validity threat,
ethical issue, feasibility constraint, and expected contribution.

Offer multiple design options when trade-offs are material. Explain what each
option can and cannot establish. Keep the proposed contribution proportional to
the evidence gap; novelty alone is not a contribution.
