# Evidence audit guide

Use this guide to calibrate claims, not to assign a universal score to every
source. A lower-tier source can be the correct source for a fact about a public
announcement, while a media article is not sufficient evidence for a causal
scientific conclusion.

## Practical source tiers

| Tier | Typical source | Best use | Typical caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Original research article, primary dataset, experiment record, official law/policy/statistic, standard, technical specification | Establishing what was measured, decided, released, or observed | Methods, coverage, version, and author incentives still require review |
| B | Systematic review, meta-analysis, major institutional report, registry, authoritative technical documentation | Synthesizing a field or documenting an established practice | Scope and inclusion criteria may not match the current question |
| C | Peer-reviewed review, scholarly book, rigorous working paper, specialist synthesis | Definitions, theory, historical development, interpretation | May be selective, dated, or one step removed from the data |
| D | Professional journalism, trade publication, expert commentary, organization webpage | Discovery, context, announcements, accessible explanations | May omit methods, uncertainty, or the original evidence |
| E | Blog, social post, forum, unsourced summary, AI-generated text | Leads and terminology for further checking | Do not use alone for consequential claims |

Tier is only one dimension. Evaluate traceability, methodological transparency,
fit, recency, independence, and known corrections or retractions as separate
dimensions.

## Claim-to-evidence procedure

1. Split a paragraph into atomic claims. A sentence containing a number, causal
   interpretation, comparison, or forecast usually contains more than one claim.
2. Ask what kind of evidence the claim requires: definition, observation,
   prevalence, association, causation, mechanism, prediction, evaluation, or
   normative judgment.
3. Locate the original evidence and record its exact location.
4. Compare the source's population, setting, dates, measures, design, and
   uncertainty with the wording of the claim.
5. Check at least one independent source when the claim is central, disputed,
   high stakes, rapidly changing, or based on a single estimate.
6. Rewrite the claim to match the evidence. Narrow the population, replace
   causal language with associational language, add uncertainty, or mark it as
   an inference when needed.

## Status vocabulary

- `Verified`: the inspected source directly supports the stated claim within
  its scope.
- `Partially supported`: the source supports only part of the wording or the
  claim overreaches its population, method, or certainty.
- `Inference`: a reasoned interpretation that is not directly stated or tested
  by the source.
- `Unsupported`: no adequate evidence was located.
- `Contradicted`: credible evidence materially conflicts with the claim.
- `Requires verification`: the source or relevant evidence could not be
  inspected.

Never use “consensus” merely because several sources repeat the same statement.
Check whether they are independent and whether a review, primary dataset, or
counterexample changes the picture.
